The Philosophy

I ask why a lot.

I was trained as a journalist, which means I was trained to believe that no story is self-explanatory. Someone has to do the work of finding the essential thing in a complex situation and making it legible to someone who wasn't in the room. That instinct doesn't leave you when the subject shifts to a router manual or an API endpoint; if anything, it becomes more important. The difference between documentation that users trust and documentation they abandon is usually a question of editorial judgment as much as technical accuracy.

Before the content operations work, I spent years as a field service technician; on-site, under pressure, diagnosing failures in industrial hydraulic equipment and high-speed mail processing systems for customers across North America, South America, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia. That kind of work teaches you to think in systems at the level where abstraction is a liability. You can't fake understanding how something actually works when it's broken and someone needs it fixed. That grounding has stayed with me through every role since: I approach a CCMS migration or a publication workflow problem the same way I approached a hydraulic circuit fault. Find where the system actually is, figure out why it's there, and build toward where it should be.

In my own time I go deep on ambitious attempts to break the mold. IBM's Workplace OS experiments in the 90s. The Honda NR750 and its oval pistons. Sega's early swings at online gaming with the Saturn and Dreamcast. What draws me to all of it is consistent: someone tried to do something genuinely different, and the distance between what they attempted and what happened is worth understanding; often more so than the straightforward successes.

Whether it's a 20,000-topic knowledge base that AI systems can't reach, or a publication cycle that takes three weeks when it should take one day, or a hydraulic cylinder that's leaking for no obvious reason; I want to understand it at the source, find the gap between how it is and how it could be, and close that gap if I can.

Systems in Action

Solving for Scale

Architecture

Architecting an API-Driven Content Engine

NETGEAR

The Problem: Documentation was siloed in a legacy on-premises CCMS that couldn't integrate with modern delivery systems or AI-assisted tools, creating a growing bottleneck as the product portfolio expanded.

The System: Led end-to-end migration of 20,000+ DITA XML topics from the legacy DITAToo platform to SaaS Heretto; building an API-driven, single-source-of-truth content layer designed for structured delivery to AI chatbots, voice bots, and support platforms.

The Result: Static documentation library transformed into a live input layer for AI-assisted customer support. The content architecture is designed to serve six downstream platforms without duplication or drift.

Operations

Building the Operational Infrastructure

NETGEAR

The Problem: A lack of high-fidelity data made it impossible to identify systemic bottlenecks, plan capacity accurately, or demonstrate the team's output to leadership.

The System: Architected a custom Jira-based project management ecosystem from the ground up to capture real-time team velocity, throughput, and capacity data across a globally distributed team.

The Result: Transformed a reactive operation into a data-driven one; establishing defensible baselines for planning, enabling resource requests that could be backed up with numbers, and making invisible work visible.

Efficiency

Slashing Time-to-Publish by 95%

NETGEAR

The Problem: Legacy publication cycles carried excessive cross-functional overhead and hard dependencies on other teams, taking 2–3 weeks to ship even minor content updates.

The System: Reengineered the end-to-end publication workflow to remove external dependencies and give the documentation team direct ownership of releases; a true continuous delivery model.

The Result: Publication cycle reduced from 3 weeks to a single day. Content ships at the speed of the product, not the speed of a cross-functional scheduling queue.

Leadership

Rebuilding a Team Under Fire

NETGEAR

The Problem: Severe team attrition created dangerous single points of failure across critical documentation functions, placing product launch timelines and compliance requirements at risk.

The System: Cross-training frameworks to maintain coverage during the gap, then a strategic hiring effort; two writers positioned specifically to eliminate the single-contributor dependencies that had made the team fragile.

The Result: 100% operational continuity with zero launch delays throughout the attrition period. Team rebuilt to a more resilient foundation than before.

Off the Clock

The curiosities that connect everything else.

The '92 Porsche 968 in my garage is a useful window into how I think. It's a technically accomplished car that arrived at the wrong moment, was priced below several less capable Porsches, and was consequently dismissed by most of the market. The gap between what it actually is and how it's perceived is, to me, genuinely interesting. I've spent significant time on a rotating cast of strange vehicles that made brave and often commercially unsuccessful choices. I've owned Mazda rotaries. I've owned Renaults almost nobody in the US has heard of. Ambitious attempts at doing things differently tend to teach you more than the straightforward successes, and I find them proportionally more interesting.

My music taste runs toward things that reward serious attention over casual familiarity. Silver Jews and Smog for literary, devastatingly precise songwriting. Sonic Youth, Polvo, Mission of Burma, and Lync for the ways they found to use rock instrumentation against itself. Portishead, Acetone, Boards of Canada for the slower and more deliberate kind of weight. Anything that gets better the fifth time than it was the first.

I see a lot of films at the Roxie, the independent theater in the Mission District where I'm a member; David Lynch, Hal Hartley, Miranda July, Sofia Coppola, anything where the form is being used to do something a different medium couldn't. Fill in the rest with good food, interesting bars, video games that reward thought over reflexes, and an enthusiasm for understanding how things work that I genuinely cannot seem to turn off. The Resident Evil series in particular; I went deep enough into an undocumented music bug in the PC HD Remaster that I wrote up the fix myself, and that guide has been live on Steam ever since. When something isn't explained and it should be, I end up explaining it.
